# Persona 5: Young Professional (Professional Services)

## Profile
- **Name**: Alex Thompson
- **Age**: 26
- **Role**: Second-year Analyst at a Big Four management consulting firm (Strategy & Operations practice). Staffed on a mix of process-improvement and digital transformation engagements across healthcare and retail clients.
- **Education**: B.A. in Economics from the University of Michigan, minor in Statistics.
- **Location**: Chicago, IL — rents a one-bedroom in River North for $2,100/month with a roommate who splits the cost. Close enough to walk to the office when the weather cooperates, which it rarely does.
- **Income**: ~$95K all-in (base ~$85K + year-end performance bonus). After Illinois taxes and a modest 401(k) contribution, his take-home is comfortable but not lavish. Acutely aware of when his next performance review is.
- **Lifestyle**: Driven by the rhythm of client engagements. When he's on a project, his life becomes the project — late nights in hotel rooms building decks, early morning calls with client stakeholders, and weekend email triage. When a project "wraps," he has a brief window of relative calm before the staffing team assigns him to the next one. He travels roughly 40–50% of the time (Mon–Thu on-site at the client, home Fri–Sun). Dresses business casual in the office, business formal on-site with conservative clients.
- **Key Traits**: Detail-oriented under pressure, fast learner, quietly competitive with his cohort. Projects confidence in client-facing settings but is privately anxious about whether he's developing fast enough. Has internalized the consulting mindset of "structured thinking" — he genuinely frames problems as 2x2 matrices and issue trees, even in his personal life.

## Daily Routine & Pain Points

- **Monday (Travel Day)**: Alarm at 5 AM. Uber to O'Hare, a gate-side coffee, and two hours of deck edits on the flight. On-site by early afternoon. First client meeting by 2 PM. Dinner with the project team (expensed), then two more hours of work in the hotel before sleep. Every flight is a mini work sprint.
- **Tue–Thu (On-Site)**: 8 AM at the client office. Days are a mix of stakeholder interviews, workshop facilitation, and data analysis back at his laptop. Evenings are deck-building and manager review cycles, often until 10–11 PM in the hotel business center or his room.
- **Friday (Home Day)**: Works from home or the Chicago office. Catches up on internal admin, staffing conversations, and anything not client-facing. Usually wraps by 6–7 PM — which feels luxurious by comparison. Tries to exercise and recharge.
- **Weekend**: Nominally off. In practice, Saturday mornings involve a quick Slack check and at least one "quick" email that isn't quick. Sundays have a creeping dread of the Monday flight. He protects Friday and Saturday nights for socializing.
- **Pain Points**:
  - **Deck fatigue**: He spends an enormous portion of his working life in PowerPoint. Any tool that can reduce "slide polish" time is immediately compelling.
  - **Context switching**: Jumping between two or three active workstreams with different clients, datasets, and stakeholder personalities is cognitively exhausting.
  - **Data cleanup drudgery**: A significant portion of "analysis" is actually cleaning and reformatting messy client Excel exports before anything interesting can be done with them. He finds this deeply unsatisfying.
  - **Client confidentiality constraints**: His firm has strict data handling policies. He cannot paste client data into any non-approved external tool. This is not optional — a compliance violation would derail his career.
  - **The output quality trap**: His manager reviews everything before it goes to the client. He needs AI output to be a credible starting draft, not something that requires more time to fix than to write from scratch.
  - **Personal life compression**: Travel means he misses friends' events, gym routines, and the basic continuity of home life. He is beginning to quietly question the lifestyle, though he is not ready to leave yet.

## Perspective on AI-Native Products

- **Output must be client-ready or near-client-ready**: He does not have time to substantially rewrite AI-generated text. If it requires more editing than writing from scratch, it fails the value test.
- **Structure over creativity**: He thinks in bullet points, frameworks, and executive summaries. He wants AI that can produce structured, scannable outputs — not flowing prose. A three-slide narrative arc, not a short story.
- **Confidentiality is the first filter**: Before evaluating any tool's capabilities, he asks whether using it would violate his firm's data policy. For anything touching client data (even indirectly), the answer for non-approved tools is no.
- **Consistency and reliability matter more than peak performance**: A tool that is brilliant 80% of the time and hallucinating 20% of the time is a professional liability. He would rather use a slightly worse tool he can trust consistently.
- **Efficiency gains, not magic**: He is not looking for AI to "transform" his work. He is looking for it to shave two hours off a slide deck or get a first-draft interview summary out in five minutes instead of forty-five.
- **Peer adoption signals legitimacy**: He pays attention to what tools his manager and senior colleagues are using. If a partner mentions using a tool in a meeting, he'll have it trialed by end of week.

- **Untapped Potential**: He knows AI tools exist that could dramatically accelerate data cleaning and Excel automation (e.g., coding assistants that write Python/VBA for data pipelines) but hasn't invested the time to learn them properly. It's on his mental "when I have bandwidth" list, which never materializes.

## Decision Criteria for New Tools

1. **Compliance-safe**: If there is any ambiguity about whether a tool violates firm data policy, he won't risk it. His career is not worth a shortcut.
2. **Immediate time savings on high-frequency tasks**: The tool must address something he does constantly — not occasionally. Slide drafting, meeting summaries, and email drafting are high-frequency. He doesn't want a solution to a rare problem.
3. **Output quality floor**: The worst output the tool produces must still be a credible starting point. He has no tolerance for confident nonsense.
4. **Low setup friction**: He will not spend more than an hour configuring a personal tool. If it requires significant onboarding, he will abandon it before he sees value.
5. **Data privacy clarity**: He reads the data handling summary before connecting any account. He will not link work credentials or client-adjacent data to third-party tools.
6. **Social proof from respected sources**: Recommendations from senior colleagues, reputable tech newsletters, or people in professional services he follows online carry significant weight.
